## Why the first runway script was hard to follow

The old S2 voiceover was:

> You earn one lakh a month and want two years of runway. Spend eighty thousand, and you need nineteen lakh. Saving twenty thousand a month, that takes eight years. Now spend fifty thousand. You need twelve lakh, and you save fifty thousand a month. Two years. Spend less, save faster, need less…

The insight is right. The chain is not. In about 20 seconds the viewer has to hold income, a two-year goal, two spend levels, two savings rates, two goal piles (₹19.2 lakh, rounded in the voice to nineteen, and ₹12 lakh), and two wait times (eight years and two years). That is eight or more spoken quantities, plus a mid-script reset ("Now spend fifty thousand") that starts a second calculation before the first one has settled.

The real idea is one lever: **the same cut in spend shrinks the goal and speeds the saving.** The old script buried that line at the end, after the arithmetic had already been lost. The rewritten versions put that lever first, speak at most two numbers, and let the picture carry the rest of the maths.

The maths itself is correct and should stay on screen when the 80k-vs-50k example is used:

- Two years of expenses = monthly spend × 24
- Months to save = goal ÷ monthly savings
- Earn ₹1 lakh, spend ₹80,000: need ₹19.2 lakh, save ₹20,000, wait 96 months (8 years)
- Spend ₹50,000: need ₹12 lakh, save ₹50,000, wait 24 months (2 years)

Do not round ₹19.2 lakh to 19 on screen.

## Research findings

High-performing short finance and explainer reels, and the educational-video research behind them, converge on a short list of rules.

**One idea per reel.** A video that tries to teach budgeting, saving and investing is three videos. If you cannot state the insight in one sentence, it does not fit a reel. Topics that need a long chain of calculations should be a series, not a denser monologue.

**Hook in the first 1–3 seconds.** No "hey," no "in this video," no warm-up. Open with a checkable claim, a question the viewer already has, a surprising number, a common mistake, or a pain question. Ankur Warikoo often starts with a counterintuitive first line, then talks to one person, not a crowd. Ali Abdaal shorts start the payoff before 2.5–3 seconds. Write a 4–7 word on-screen hook as well, and do not make it a word-for-word copy of the spoken line. The screen and the voice should share the job, not repeat it.

**At most one or two numbers in the voice.** Finance shorts that work use one worked example, not a table read aloud. If the script runs long, cut the second spoken number first. Every other figure can live on screen. Recalculate every example before recording.

**A single chain of thought.** Each spoken line should be the next step of the line before it. No mid-script "and also," and no second scenario that forces the viewer to restart the maths. Kurzgesagt-style explainers give each idea its own visual beat, then move on. If a beat contains "and also," split it into two reels or two beats, with a pause between them.

**A concrete, relatable case.** Indian finance creators land harder with an exact rupee situation (a salary, an EMI, a phone, a car) than with a general lecture. CA Rachana Ranade often opens with an everyday analogy, then the definition, then one comparison. Sharan Hegde wraps one practical lesson in a short story or skit so the viewer feels they learned something useful, not just that they were entertained.

**A contrast or before-after, not a spreadsheet.** Show one change. Hold the other variables still. "Same salary, lower spend" is followable. "Here are two fully worked plans" is not, unless the second plan is only shown, not narrated.

**Word budget.** A clear voiceover sits around 2.3 to 2.6 words per second, or about 140–160 words a minute (150 wpm is a common planning figure, about 2.5 words per second). For a 25–30 second reel that is **about 60–75 spoken words**, including the CTA, with a little air for pauses. Hit the pace by cutting words, not by rushing the read. Slow slightly on the payoff line.

**Let the visuals carry the numbers.** Cognitive-load research on educational video (Mayer's multimedia principles, as summarised by Brame) says the voice and the picture should share the work. The picture should show the mechanism, not decorate the narration. Remove clutter (weeding). Give each idea its own moment (segmenting). Put a number on screen when it matters, with a unit, and do not also recite a full table. Captions stay short: about 1–5 words on screen at a time.

**Payoff, then CTA.** The last teaching line restates the hook's promise in plain words and adds no new claim. End on that completed graphic for a beat, then the end card. The spoken CTA is short and always the same.

**What to steal from specific creators, without copying them:**

- **Ankur Warikoo:** a sharp first line, one person address, a clear promise, a reflective close rather than a hard sell. For us the waitlist CTA still comes last, but the payoff line before it should feel like a thought the viewer can keep.
- **CA Rachana Ranade:** one concept, a familiar analogy, plain words, a single comparison, a calm close.
- **Sharan Hegde (Finance with Sharan):** one practical nugget, told as a story; the viewer should leave able to use the idea the same day.
- **Ali Abdaal shorts:** hook with no preamble; something on screen changes every few seconds; end on the payoff frame, not a long outro.
- **Kurzgesagt-style explainers:** personal hook, one drawable scene per beat, visual metaphor for abstract ideas, a reflective close. On-screen text is labels, not paragraphs.

## Rules

1. One idea. If there are two insights, make two reels.
2. One chain of thought. Each line follows from the line before.
3. Hook first. The first sentence is the promise. No preamble.
4. Speak at most two numbers. All other figures go on screen.
5. Indian context: rupees, lakh, EMI, salary day, 9to5, manager. Say "lakh" and "rupees" the way you would say them.
6. Plain words. No jargon, no em dashes, no "so basically," no "in this video."
7. 60–75 spoken words, timed out loud. Cut from the middle, never from the hook or the payoff.
8. The picture does the arithmetic. The voice says what it means.
9. One main element on screen at a time. Plenty of empty space. This is also the moodboard rule.
10. On-screen hook: 4–7 words, not a duplicate of the voice.
11. Payoff restates the hook. Then the CTA. No new idea after the payoff.
12. Every claim must already exist in the book, a published blog, or `facts.md`.
13. Recheck the maths before recording. Show ₹19.2 lakh, not 19, when that figure is used.
14. Number reels can carry a small on-screen line: "Educational, not financial advice." Do not spend voiceover time on it.

## S2 template (number / data-viz reels)

Use S2 when the idea is a formula, a lever, or one worked rupee example.

| Beat | Time | Spoken job | Picture job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0–3 s | One claim, question, or surprising cost | 4–7 word title. One object. |
| Setup | 3–8 s | Name the one idea in plain words | The object or formula begins to build |
| One change | 8–18 s | Describe the single lever or the single example. At most two spoken numbers. | The number the voice is talking about is the only one in motion. Other figures can sit still, small, as labels. |
| Payoff | 18–24 s | What it means, in one short line that answers the hook | The before-after completes. Hold it. |
| CTA | last 5–6 s | The standard close | End card: book cover, January 2027, quit9to5.in |

Do not narrate both a full "before" calculation and a full "after" calculation. Show the first state, change one thing, and let both sides of the picture move.

**S2 skeleton:**

> [Hook: the mistake or the hidden lever.]
> [Plain name of the idea.]
> [One example or one change. Two numbers, or fewer.]
> [What both sides of the picture just did.]
> [Payoff in one line.]
> From my book, Quit 9to5. Join the waitlist at quit9to5.in.

## S3 template (kinetic type / concept reels)

Use S3 when the idea is a named framework or a reframe (Alignment Tax, money as a painkiller, irreplaceability). Numbers stay off the voice, or appear only if they are already part of a short, famous line.

| Beat | Time | Spoken job | Picture job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0–3 s | A feeling or claim the viewer recognises | One bold word or object already in motion |
| Name the cost | 3–10 s | What it really is, in plain words | One metaphor builds (a stamp, a wedge, a pill) |
| Mechanism | 10–18 s | The one cause, in a single chain | The metaphor does one thing. No extra icons. |
| Name it | 18–22 s | The book name, if there is one | The title lands. Hold. |
| Payoff | 22–25 s | The reframe, no new claim | The image resolves (arrows align, the dose is put down) |
| CTA | last 5–6 s | The standard close | End card, same as S2 |

Do not list three symptoms in a row if one image can stand for the cost. Lists break the chain.

**S3 skeleton:**

> [Hook: the feeling, said simply.]
> [What it is, and what it is not.]
> [How it shows up in a day or a week. One cause.]
> [The book name, if the idea has one.]
> [Payoff reframe.]
> From my book, Quit 9to5. Join the waitlist at quit9to5.in.

## Visual rules (for the script's beat sheet)

These exist because the first moodboard felt cluttered.

- One main element at a time. A second label is fine. A second story is not.
- Leave empty space. If a frame looks busy, remove an object, do not shrink everything.
- A number appears when the voice reaches that idea, not in a cluster at the start.
- On-screen text is short labels (3–7 words), not a transcript.
- Captions: 1–5 words on screen, one highlight colour on the operative word.
- Something is moving every second, but movement is one object, not five.
- End on the completed payoff graphic for about a second, then the end card.

## Pallav's rule (20 Nov 2026): start from the viewer's goal, never from jargon
Hook = the outcome the viewer wants ("Want to quit your 9to5 faster?"). Then each line answers the question the previous line raises: goal -> what it needs (savings) -> how to get it fast (spend less) -> why it works twice (save more monthly AND need less) -> one example -> payoff -> CTA. Never open with a term the viewer hasn't been given a reason to care about (e.g. "runway"); introduce it only after the why, or skip it.

### Approved-direction runway script (3-year example, math checked)
"Want to quit your 9to5 faster? First, you need money saved. Enough to cover your expenses while you build something of your own. Say three years' worth. So how do you save it quickly? Spend less. And that works twice. One, you save more every month. Two, the amount you need shrinks, because you're planning around a smaller monthly bill. Earn one lakh and spend eighty thousand? That's twelve years away. Spend fifty thousand? Just three. Same salary. From my book, Quit 9to5. Join the waitlist at quit9to5.in."
On screen: 80k x 36 = ₹28.8 lakh at ₹20k/month = 144 months = 12 years; 50k x 36 = ₹18 lakh at ₹50k/month = 36 months = 3 years.

## Retention rules (Instagram research, 21 Nov 2026)

Full method: [`INSTAGRAM_SCRIPT_METHOD.md`](./INSTAGRAM_SCRIPT_METHOD.md). Sources are listed in `reels/003_loans/SCRIPT.md` on the box (not included).

1. **What Instagram scores:** watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach (Mosseri). Skip rate (leaving in < 3 s) is listed first in insights. For reaching new people, write for **sends**: every reel needs one line someone would forward.
2. **First frame = headline.** The hook is spoken AND on screen in frame 1, already moving, with the stake visible (one object). Instagram suggests a question in the first frame and the answer a few seconds later.
3. **One open loop, paid off late.** Set it in the first 3–7 s ("two things", "the one nobody counts"), show a counter, pay it off after ~70% of the runtime. Never cheat it with "link in bio" as the answer.
4. **Re-hook around 10–14 s** with a pattern interrupt (hard cut, new object, counter tick) plus an escalation line. Something on screen changes every 2–4 s.
5. **End on the payoff, then the CTA, then a held end frame (~0.4 s)** that mirrors or loops back to frame 1 (e.g. the lock from frame 1 opens in the last teaching beat).
6. **Length:** 30–38 s is fine for one worked example (75–95 words incl. CTA); 25–30 s (60–75 words) for a pure reframe. No ideal length, but every second must earn the next.
7. **Works muted and stands alone** for someone who has never seen the account.
8. **CTA override:** the S2/S3 skeletons above end with the old "quit9to5.in" CTA. Use the current mandatory CTA instead: "My book Quit 9to5 is coming out soon. Join the waitlist in bio." (no URL).
9. **From the LinkedIn Harness (inspiration only):** first-person stance lines are Pallav's strongest openers and make good proof beats or alt hooks; line 2 adds a detail; clickbait formulas underperform. Don't import the "…more" fold, long text, or link-in-comment habits.
